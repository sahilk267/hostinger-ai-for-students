## Source A — NAEYC developmentally appropriate assessment
URL: https://www.naeyc.org/resources/position-statements/dap/assessing-development

NAEYC says assessment for young children should be ongoing, strategic, reflective, purposeful, developmentally/culturally/linguistically responsive and based on authentic activities, observations and work samples. It warns that children develop unevenly and may demonstrate skills differently across contexts; standardized results should be reliable/valid for the population and not used for high-stakes decisions when limits are known. It also says screening is not diagnosis or labeling, major decisions should use multiple sources and family consultation, and digitally based assessment should be limited for young children.

Product implication: ages 5–8 should use short play-based, multimodal observations and parent/educator context, produce strengths/support language, and never output IQ, diagnosis, fixed ability or career destiny.

## Source B — FTC COPPA rule summary
URL: https://www.ftc.gov/legal-library/browse/rules/childrens-online-privacy-protection-rule-coppa

COPPA applies to services directed to children under 13, or services with actual knowledge that they collect personal information online from children under 13. Product implication: if serving younger children, the platform needs a dedicated child privacy design, clear parent notice/consent and strict limits on collection and sharing; it should not quietly treat a child account like an adult account.

## Source C — India Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023
URL: https://www.meity.gov.in/static/uploads/2024/06/2bf1f0e9f04e6fb4f8fef35e82c42aa5.pdf

The primary Act text includes Section 9 on processing children’s personal data, including verifiable parental consent and additional restrictions around harmful processing. Product implication: an India-based child feature must be legally reviewed before launch, use verified parent control, minimize child data, avoid profiling/targeted monetization and provide deletion/withdrawal paths. This is product/legal research, not a legal compliance opinion.

## Source D — UNICEF play guidance
URL: https://www.unicef.org/parenting/child-care/what-is-free-play

UNICEF’s guidance describes play as a way children explore, express emotions, build vocabulary, problem-solve, develop independence and use imagination. It emphasizes children’s freedom to choose and adults giving clues rather than doing the work for them. Product implication: ages 5–8 should use playful, choice-rich, story-based activities with parent co-play and observation—not timed cognitive ranking tests.

## Source E — Harvard Center on the Developing Child executive-function activities
URL: https://developingchild.harvard.edu/resources/handouts-tools/activities-guide-enhancing-and-practicing-executive-function-skills/

Harvard presents executive function/self-regulation as skills that can develop through interaction and practice, with age-appropriate games for 5–7, 7–12 and adolescents. The skills are observable through activities involving planning, attention, working memory, flexible thinking and self-regulation, but the guide is for support and practice rather than diagnosis. Product implication: games can report “observed practice signals” in these skills and recommend activities, but must not convert a single performance into a fixed trait.

## Source F — National Academies adolescent development
URL: https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK545476/

The National Academies describe adolescence as a period of profound biological, cognitive, psychosocial and emotional change, with substantial developmental plasticity and growing agency that still requires scaffolding and support. Early experiences influence but do not determine later outcomes. Product implication: ages 13–17 should emphasize learner choice, identity exploration, critical judgment and supported experimentation; reports must not treat current gameplay as a stable destiny.

## Source G — Washington State Career Guidance Washington
URL: https://ospi.k12.wa.us/student-success/graduation/career-guidance-washington-lessons

The official school curriculum frames career guidance for Grades 6–12 as portfolio development, ownership of learning, identifying strengths and interests, researching possible career areas and connecting interests to possibilities. It is exploratory and plan-oriented, not a one-test verdict. Product implication: parent reports should say “possible directions to explore” and include the child’s own interests, artifacts and next experiments.
